\begin{abstract}
We identify a phase diagram governing resonator-based decoding in Fourier Holographic Reduced Representations, controlled by $\rho = \frac{\text{distinct codevectors per block}}{\text{block dimensionality}}$. Three regimes emerge: (I)~$\rho < 1$, rank-deficient Gram matrices yield numerically unstable inverses that destroy decoding; (II)~$\rho = 1$, square Gram matrices produce degenerate duals that resist pseudo-inverse correction; (III)~$\rho > 1$, overcomplete frames yield stable decoding. We anchor regime~II in random-matrix theory: the Gram matrix is a scaled square Wishart, whose smallest eigenvalue touches the soft edge at the origin and whose condition number scales as $\kappa \sim 4n^{2}$, a prediction we verify across $n \in [32, 512]$. Empirically, the collapse is governed by the physical condition number: decoders fail exactly when $\kappa(M)$ enters the critical band $[10^{3}, 10^{4}]$, with $\kappa^{*} = 5.81\times 10^{3}$ the interpolated 50\% point at $n=32$. The law transfers across algebras (HRR, BSC, MAP) in a refined two-factor form: failure requires the critical \emph{band} in $\kappa$ \emph{and} a closed-loop observer to amplify the projected noise. Pure resonators and learned decoders are unaffected in all regimes --- the collapse belongs to the observer, not the representation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In vector symbolic architectures (VSA), the ``binary collapse'' phenomenon at high superposition has been reported anecdotally for decades \cite{plate1995holographic, frady2020resonator}. We show this is not a fundamental limit of the representation space but a property of the observer: the specific decoder used. When the Gram matrix is inverted on a $\rho=1$ (square) block, the operator becomes singular; when a neural network is trained instead, the same vector is recoverable with 99\% accuracy.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{FHRR and HRR.} FHRR (Fourier Holographic Reduced Representations) uses complex unit vectors with phase binding \cite{plate1995holographic}. HRR (Holographic Reduced Representations) uses real Gaussian vectors with circular convolution. The resonator network \cite{frady2020resonator} factorizes bound structures by iterative competition between codebooks.

\paragraph{Gram matrix decoding.} For $n$ codevectors of dimension $d$ in a block, the Gram matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ with $M_{ij} = \langle c_i, c_j \rangle$ determines whether inverse-based decoding succeeds. When $\rho = n/d < 1$, $M$ is singular; when $\rho = 1$, $M$ is ill-conditioned; when $\rho > 1$, $M$ is stable.

\paragraph{Decoders compared.} We test four decoders: (i) \textbf{gram} (custom inverse), (ii) \textbf{pure} (no Gram correction), (iii) \textbf{pinv} (pseudo-inverse with $rcond=10^{-10}$), and (iv) \textbf{gradient} (continuous optimization).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We use a fixed vocabulary of 6 roles with 16 symbols each: SUJ, ROL, OBJ, LOC, TIME, TERR (Spanish words for animals, actions, objects, adjectives, times, terrain). Facts are generated with fixed seeds (BlockBundle=7, make\_fact=42). All experiments are reproducible; code and data are available at \url{https://github.com/Rylow999/fhrr-rho-collapse}.

\paragraph{Grid.} We vary $\rho$ by changing block dimensionality $BLK$ while keeping the codebook size fixed at 96 distinct codevectors (6 roles × 16 symbols). The coarse grid spans $\rho \in [0.727, 1.500]$ across experiments (Exp.~H2/V3); the reported fine structure uses $\rho \in [0.727, 1.231]$ (Exp.~9).

\paragraph{Fine sweep.} For $\rho \approx 1$ we use a finer grid of 14 values around 1.00, with 10 random seeds and 20 facts per seed.

The phase diagram shows three regimes:
\begin{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-algebra validation: HRR, BSC, and attention mechanisms}

\textbf{HRR real} (Gaussian phases). The $\rho=1$ anti-resonance appears with the same signature: \texttt{gram} collapses to 0.05, \texttt{pinv} to 0.13, \texttt{pure} maintains 0.98. The mechanism is identical: square Gram singular at exactly $\rho=1$.

\textbf{Binary Spatter Codes (BSC).} The $\rho=1$ transition holds in binary VSA (Exp.~11b, 8 seeds $\times$ 25 facts per cell, \texttt{data/exp11\_bsc\_rho.json}): at the square point (K=3 blocks, BLK=32, $n_{cv}=32$) \texttt{gram} collapses to $0.214$ and \texttt{pinv} to $0.244$, while \texttt{pure} maintains $0.988$. Two facts extend the FHRR result beyond a qualitative analogy. First, the median Gram condition number at the square point is $\kappa = 4.7\times 10^{3}$ --- inside the same critical band $[10^{3},10^{4}]$ identified for FHRR, so the $\kappa$-window (not merely the $\rho$-locus) transfers across algebras. Second, in the rank-deficient regime $\rho>1$ the truncated Gram ($\kappa \sim 10^{15}$) recovers decoding ($0.97$--$0.98$), reproducing the characteristic U-shape. The codevectors being $\{\pm1\}^{d}$ rather than Gaussian means the Wishart anchoring is replaced by its binary counterpart (Gram of i.i.d.\ Rademacher vectors, whose spectral measure obeys the same Marchenko--Pastur law up to the fourth cumulant); the agreement of the critical band across the two ensembles is the empirical content of the universality claim.

\textbf{MAP (Multiply-Add-Permute).} MAP with per-role permutation binding (Exp.~11c, 8 seeds $\times$ 25 facts, \texttt{data/exp11c\_map\_rho.json}) refines the law in an unexpected way. In MAP, Gram decoding is \emph{single-shot} least squares over the mixing matrix $A = [P_1 C_1 \,|\, P_2 C_2]$ of the block: there is no closed-loop iteration. The collapse pattern reflects this: at $\rho = 1$ the median condition number is $\kappa = 1.06\times 10^{4}$ --- inside the critical band --- yet \texttt{gram} maintains $1.000$: the single-shot solver tolerates the band because the error is not re-injected. The collapse appears instead at $\rho > 1$ (rank-deficient), where \texttt{gram} drops to $0.239$ and $0.071$ while the truncated \texttt{pinv} recovers to $\sim 1.0$ and the \texttt{pure} resonator holds at $0.97$--$0.98$. Combining with Exp.~11b gives a complete two-factor picture: decoding failure requires (i) $\kappa(M)$ in the critical band or the rank-deficient regime, \emph{and} (ii) for band values, a closed-loop observer that amplifies the projected noise across iterations. Single-shot inversion at band $\kappa$ degrades gracefully in MAP; the same matrix iterated through a resonator (FHRR, HRR, BSC) collapses.

\textbf{Transformers / attention.} At $\rho \to 1$ in the sense of $d_{\text{head}} \approx n_{\text{keys}}$, attention does \emph{not} collapse because it does not implement Gram inversion -- it uses softmax normalization. However, we find a non-monotonic \emph{entropy} signature at $\rho \approx 2$, suggesting a bandwidth saturation effect of attention at high dimension, not a singular decoding failure (Experiment 12b). The collapse-pcode is algebra-specific, not universal.

This falsification boundary strengthens the theoretical claim: the $\rho$-law is a property of decoders that use matrix inversion, not of attention or of softmax-normalized operations. $\square$

\subsection{Kappa-conditioned refinement: the physical trigger}

The $\rho = 1$ singular point corresponds not to a geometric transition but to the \emph{condition number} $\kappa(M)$ entering a critical band $\kappa \in [10^3, 10^4]$ where the decoder fails catastrophically. The observed pattern:

- $\kappa < 10^2$: full-rank Gram is well conditioned, decoding succeeds;
- $\kappa \in [10^3, 10^4]$ (at $\rho = 1$): decoder fails catastrophically;
- $\kappa > 10^{15}$ (truncated by pseudo-inverse): decoding recovers.

Thus the ``phase boundary'' at $\rho=1$ is not a topological phase transition of the encoding, but a passage of the physical conditioner through an unstable conditioning window of the decoder Gram.

\subsection{Random-matrix anchoring: why $\rho=1$ collapses at $\kappa^* \sim 10^{3\text{--}4}$}
\label{sec:rmt}

The $\rho = 1$ singular point is not an artifact of a particular codebook realisation: it is the finite-size shadow of a \emph{square Wishart ensemble}. Let $C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ stack the $n$ codevectors of a block, so the Gram matrix is $M = C C^{\top}$ with entries $C_{ij}$ of variance $1/d$ (unit-norm Gaussian codevectors). Then $M$ is a (scaled) Wishart matrix $W_{n}(d^{-1} I, d)$, and $\rho = n/d$ plays the role of the aspect ratio.

Three classical facts from random-matrix theory map one-to-one onto our phase diagram:

\paragraph{(i) $\rho > 1$ ($d < n$, under-complete).} $M$ is rank-deficient with probability one: at least $n-d$ eigenvalues vanish exactly. Gram inversion is undefined; this is regime~I.

\paragraph{(ii) $\rho = 1$ ($n = d$, square).} The Marchenko--Pastur density $\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(\lambda) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{(4-\lambda)/\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda$ has support touching the origin: the lower edge is a \emph{soft edge} at $\lambda=0$. For a square Wishart, the smallest eigenvalue scales as $\lambda_{\min} \sim \sigma^{2}/n$ (Tracy--Widom fluctuations on the scale $n^{-2/3}$ around this edge), so the condition number scales as
\begin{equation}
\kappa(M) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} \;\sim\; 4n^{2}.
\label{eq:kappa-scaling}
\end{equation}
For our setup ($n = 32$ codevectors per block, $d = \mathrm{BLK} = 32$), Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa-scaling} predicts $\kappa \sim 4.1 \times 10^{3}$. The measured condition number at the exact square point is $\kappa = 7.2 \times 10^{3}$ (median; Exp.~14), and the 50\%-accuracy threshold sits at $\kappa^{*} = 5.81 \times 10^{3}$ --- within a factor $1.4$--$2.8$ of the asymptotic prediction. The offset has the expected anatomy: the measured $\lambda_{\min} = 4.87 \times 10^{-4}$ sits a factor $\approx 2$ below the nominal scale $\sigma^{2}/n = 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ (the smallest-eigenvalue distribution of a square Wishart is skewed toward zero relative to its median scale), while $\lambda_{\max} = 3.52$ vs.\ the predicted $4$ reflects finite-$n$ bulk shrinkage.

This places the collapse on rigorous footing: $\rho = 1$ is the unique point where the Gram ensemble is \emph{critically conditioned}, $\mathbb{E}[\log \kappa]$ diverges with $n$, and the decoder operates exactly inside the window $\kappa \in [10^{3}, 10^{4}]$ where the amplified crosstalk noise $1/\lambda_{\min}$ exceeds the codebook margin $1/\sqrt{d}$.

\paragraph{Scaling in $n$: the square-Wishart prediction holds.} Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa-scaling} is a sharp, falsifiable prediction: moving to larger codebooks at $\rho=1$ should scale $\kappa$ as $4n^{2}$, with $\lambda_{\max} \to 4$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ collapsing as $n^{-2}$. Exp.~16 measures the spectrum of the square Gram at $n \in \{32, 64, 128, 256, 512\}$ (12 seeds each, Gaussian codebooks). The measured medians follow the prediction (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}): $\lambda_{\max}$ converges from below to the Marchenko--Pastur upper edge $4$ ($3.74 \to 3.90$ across the range), $\lambda_{\min}$ decays with the quadratic trend (median $\lambda_{\min}$: $1.2 \times 10^{-4}$ at $n=32$ to $1.4 \times 10^{-6}$ at $n=512$, bracketing the $n^{-2}$ prediction $1/n^{2}$ at both ends of the range), and the median condition number tracks $4n^{2}$ within a factor $2$--$13$, inside the expected spread given that $\kappa$ for square Wishart is heavy-tailed with infinite expectation \cite{edelman1988eigenvalues}. The finite-$n$ offset reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:rmt} ($n=32$, ratio $1.76$) is the small-$n$ end of this scaling, not an anomaly.

\paragraph{Critical threshold $\kappa^*$ (empirical).} In Exp.~14 (6 seeds $\times$ 12 facts per point, $n_{cv}=32$ codevectors per block, BLK swept from 27 to 40), the decoder accuracy is $\ge 0.97$ at every sampled point except $\rho = 1.000$ exactly, where it drops to $0.146$. Interpolating in $\log_{10}\kappa$ between the two points bracketing the transition --- $\kappa = 2.31\times 10^{3}$ at $\rho=0.970$ (accuracy $0.99$) and $\kappa = 1.13\times 10^{4}$ at $\rho = 1.000$ (accuracy $0.15$) --- with linear interpolation in accuracy, the 50\%-accuracy crossing lands at the $\log_{10}\kappa$ fraction $(0.99-0.50)/(0.99-0.146) = 0.58$, i.e.
$$\kappa^{*} = 5.81\times 10^{3} \quad (\log_{10}\kappa^{*} = 3.76).$$
We emphasise this is an \emph{interpolation across a point singularity}, not a resolved threshold: between $\rho = 0.970$ and $\rho = 1.000$ on a grid of BLK the transition is a single point, so the meaningful content of $\kappa^{*}$ is the \emph{scaling} $\kappa^{*} \sim 4n^{2}$ derived above, not the third significant figure. The robust, experimentally supported statement is the band: decoding fails iff $\kappa \in [10^{3}, 10^{4}]$ with full recovery on both sides.

\paragraph{(iii) $\rho < 1$ ($d > n$, over-complete).} The Wishart spectrum is bounded away from zero: $\lambda_{\min} \to \sigma^{2}(1-\sqrt{\rho})^{2} > 0$ as $n,d \to \infty$ with $n/d \to \rho < 1$. Gram inversion is stable; this is regime~III. Table~\ref{tab:wishart-check} compares the measured $\lambda_{\min}(BLK)$ against the asymptotic Marchenko--Pastur edge $(1-\sqrt{\rho})^{2}/d$. The measured values sit uniformly \emph{above} the asymptotic edge (factor $\sim 10^{2}$--$10^{3}$). This has the expected sign and is a finite-$n$ effect, not a discrepancy: at $n=32$ the distribution of $\lambda_{\min}$ concentrates well inside the bulk, and the asymptotic edge is only approached logarithmically slowly; the exact finite-$n$ expected smallest eigenvalue of a Wishart is substantially larger than its $n\to\infty$ limit when $\rho$ is close to 1. What the table establishes is (a) the monotone collapse of $\lambda_{\min}$ as $\rho \to 1^{-}$ (two orders of magnitude between $\rho=0.80$ and $\rho=0.97$), and (b) that at $\rho=1$ the measured $\lambda_{\min} = 4.87 \times 10^{-4}$ matches the soft-edge scaling $\sigma^{2}/n = 9.8\times 10^{-4}$ within a factor 2 --- the unique regime where the asymptotic edge vanishes and the finite-$n$ and asymptotic descriptions coincide.

\paragraph{Why the nonlinear decoder survives the transition.} The RMT picture also explains the survival of \texttt{pure} and of the MLP. Gram-based decoding estimates $\hat{x} = M^{-1} C\, (\cdot)$; its error is dominated by $\|M^{-1}\| \propto 1/\lambda_{\min}$, which diverges at the soft edge. The pure resonator, by contrast, never forms the inverse: it iterates the nonlinear map $v \leftarrow \mathrm{clamp}(C^{\top} v, \mathcal{C})$ whose convergence only requires that the correct fixed point be a strong attractor in the codebook metric --- a property governed by the \emph{top} of the Gram spectrum (the coherent direction), not by the soft edge. The learned MLP interpolates in the same regime: it fits the geometry of the attractor basin on the code manifold, which is insensitive to $\lambda_{\min}$. In RMT language: the collapse is a property of the \emph{resolvent} $(M - z)^{-1}$ near $z=0$; the resonator dynamics probes only the Stieltjes transform in the bulk. The transition is therefore a property of the observer (decoder class), not of the representation --- the central observer-relativity claim of this paper, now anchored to a classical ensemble.

\section{Failed Alternatives}
\label{sec:failed}

\paragraph{Gradient decoder.} Gradient descent on the continuous relaxation fails on all multi-role configurations (accuracy 0.08--0.33), regardless of $\rho$. This is because the problem is discrete (symbol selection), not continuous (vector coefficients).

\paragraph{Geometric parametrization.} Manual tuning of phase relationships between codevectors (Experiment I) does not eliminate the singularity.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The $\rho$-phase diagram reveals that \emph{capacity} in VSA is not a fixed property but depends on the decoder class. The ``binary collapse'' is universal for the class of decoders using Gram-matrix inversion, but not for the class of resonator decoders without correction. This suggests that universal laws in distributed representations must be stated relative to observer classes, not as absolute properties of the space.

\paragraph{Connection to capacity theory.} Our finding that $\rho = 1$ is a point singularity complements capacity analyses \cite{thomas2023capacity} that focus on asymptotic scaling. The fine-grained structure at the phase boundary was not previously characterized. Through the Wishart lens (Sec.~\ref{sec:rmt}), the $\rho$ axis is exactly the aspect-ratio axis of the Marchenko--Pastur law \cite{marchenko1967distribution}, and the singular point corresponds to the soft-edge-to-hard-edge crossover of the square ensemble \cite{tracy1994level, edelman1988eigenvalues}.

\paragraph{Connection to transformers.} The residual stream of transformers can be interpreted as a VSA \cite{renner2024binding}. Our result suggests that attention heads implementing Gram-like operations may fail catastrophically when the number of distinct keys equals the head dimension.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The RMT anchoring is verified across $n \in [32, 512]$ (Exp.~16), where the scaling exponents match the square-Wishart prediction; the remaining finite-size gap is the $\mathcal{O}(1)$--$\mathcal{O}(10)$ prefactor of $\kappa$, which is intrinsic to the heavy-tailed condition-number distribution at the soft edge and not a deficit of the model. We tested FHRR, HRR-real, BSC, and MAP quantitatively; sparse block codes and other bind operators are open. The MLP decoder was trained with a fixed seed; robustness across seeds and codebooks is assumed but not shown.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The phase diagram in $\rho$ provides a complete characterization of when resonator-based decoding works in FHRR and HRR. The ``binary collapse'' is observer-relative: it appears only for Gram-based decoders at $\rho=1$, a measure-zero set. Pure resonators and learned decoders are unaffected. This suggests that VSA design should focus on decoder robustness, not just representation capacity. Code and data are available at \url{https://github.com/Rylow999/fhrr-rho-collapse}.
